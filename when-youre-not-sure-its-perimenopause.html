Is it perimenopause? What to do when you’re not sure

Something has changed and you can’t quite name it. You don’t need a label to get help. Here’s how to make sense of it, and what to do next.

26 October 20265 min read8 references

In brief

  • You don’t need a label to ask for help.
  • Tiredness and many other changes have more than one possible explanation.
  • Heavier bleeding deserves a conversation. Any bleeding after menopause should be checked.

You might recognise yourself in a description of perimenopause, or only in part of it. Perhaps your periods have changed, but you’re sleeping well. Perhaps concentration feels harder, while your periods seem familiar. Or several things have shifted at once, and you’re wondering whether they belong together.

That question is worth taking to a healthcare professional just as it is. You don’t need to turn it into a diagnosis before you ask.

A transition, not a checklist

Perimenopause is the transition leading towards menopause. Menopause itself is usually recognised looking back, after 12 months without a period, when a clinician looks at the whole picture. Hormonal contraception can change bleeding patterns, so a missing period isn’t always a straightforward answer. That’s why a clinician weighs those details, rather than treating a date on the calendar as a diagnosis.1–3

Experiences vary widely. Changes to periods, hot flushes, night sweats, sleep, mood and concentration can all happen around this transition, but nobody has all of them. Someone else’s story can give you useful words without becoming a template for your body.1,3

Familiar symptoms, more than one explanation

Many of these experiences aren’t specific to menopause. Tiredness, for example, can come with disrupted sleep, a demanding stretch of life, illness or medicines, and tiredness that doesn’t go away can need checking for another health condition. An overlap is a reason to stay curious, not a reason to decide that hormones explain everything.4,5

Two changes arriving together are worth mentioning together. That doesn’t prove one caused the other, or that both share a cause. You can describe what you noticed and leave the explanation open.

Compare two notes. “I’m waking more often, and it’s harder to concentrate the next afternoon” describes what happened. “My estrogen is causing my brain fog” makes a claim a note can’t prove.

Start with what has changed for you

If you’d like to prepare, choose a few details you can easily remember: what changed, roughly when it began, whether it comes and goes, and what it gets in the way of. “I keep losing the thread in meetings” can say more than a long list of symptom names. There’s no score to keep, and no need to track every day.

What can help in the meantime

You don’t have to wait for an answer to start looking after yourself. NHS guidance for perimenopause and menopause symptoms suggests some everyday steps:6

  • keep to regular rest and sleep routines
  • eat a healthy, balanced diet
  • exercise regularly, including weight-bearing exercise, which can help protect your bones
  • try something relaxing, such as yoga, tai chi or meditation
  • talk to other people going through the same thing, like family, friends or colleagues

Talk to a doctor before taking herbal supplements or complementary medicines. And if you still need contraception, ask about it: HRT is not contraception.6

Uncertainty doesn’t mean waiting

Contact a healthcare professional if changes worry you or are affecting everyday life. Bleeding that becomes heavier deserves a conversation too. And any bleeding after menopause should be checked, even a small amount, even once. It shouldn’t be assumed to be an ordinary part of the transition.1,7,8

One useful question: “Could perimenopause be part of this, and what else should we consider?” It leaves room for evidence, for your circumstances and for more than one explanation, and you can ask it before you have a label for any of it.
