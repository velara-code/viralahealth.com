Sleep and menopause: what’s going on, and what can help

Hard nights can have more than one cause. Here’s what’s known, what you can try tonight, and when it’s worth getting checked.

26 October 20266 min read5 references

In brief

  • Hard nights often have more than one cause.
  • Small changes to sleep habits are the first thing to try tonight.
  • Breathing pauses, gasping or heavy daytime tiredness need assessment. Never drive when sleepy.

Knowing that sleep can change around menopause is one thing. Knowing why you were awake at three o’clock last night is another, and that takes more than a symptom label.

If your nights have become difficult, you don’t have to choose between “it must be menopause” and “it can’t be menopause.” A healthcare conversation can look at the transition alongside everything else that might be playing a part.

What’s known, and what stays individual

Difficulty falling asleep or staying asleep can happen during perimenopause and menopause. Night sweats can make sleep problems worse, and poor sleep can bring irritability and difficulty concentrating, which helps explain why nights and days can feel so linked. None of this tells you the cause of any one person’s symptoms.1

Sleep guidance also recognises other causes: stress, pain, medicines, caffeine, shift work and your bedroom itself, among others. Several can matter at the same time. A demanding schedule doesn’t make a physical symptom less real, and a hot flush doesn’t rule out a separate sleep problem.2

Describe the night before explaining it

It can help to describe the pattern in plain words. Is falling asleep the hard part, or waking after you’ve dropped off? Do you remember feeling hot or sweaty? Is it discomfort that wakes you, or are you not sure? And what is the next day like?

“I woke three times and felt hot twice” keeps what you noticed separate from what you assume. “Every awakening was hormonal” closes the question before anyone has explored it. If you didn’t notice what woke you, “I don’t know” is useful too. An uncertain detail doesn’t need to become a definite one.

You don’t need to keep a detailed diary. A few examples can be enough to explain a concern: choose the details that show the disruption, rather than reconstructing every minute of the night.

What can help tonight

Insomnia usually gets better by changing sleep habits. These are the NHS’s suggestions; one or two is a good place to start.2

  • go to bed only when you feel sleepy, and get up at the same time every day
  • give yourself an hour to wind down before bed, with a bath or a book, for example
  • keep your bedroom dark, quiet and comfortable; curtains, an eye mask or ear plugs can help
  • exercise regularly during the day, but not in the 4 hours before bed
  • avoid caffeine, alcohol and smoking for 6 hours before bed, and big meals late at night
  • put your phone and other screens away before bed
  • skip daytime naps, and don’t sleep in after a bad night: keep to your usual hours

If night sweats or hot flushes wake you, the NHS suggests wearing lightweight clothing, keeping your bedroom cool, and a cool shower, a fan or a cold drink. Finding ways to lower your stress, and avoiding triggers such as spicy food, caffeine, hot drinks, smoking and alcohol, can help too.3

Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT), a type of talking therapy, can help with sleep problems and with hot flushes. A GP may offer it, face to face or as an online programme.2,3

When to get it checked

Sleep difficulties that continue, are hard to cope with, or haven’t improved with changes you’ve already tried deserve professional attention. Mention how they affect your days, and any medicines you take. No article can tell you whether menopause-related care, assessment for another sleep condition or something else is right for you.2

Breathing symptoms matter especially. Pauses in breathing, gasping or choking during sleep, or persistent heavy daytime tiredness warrant assessment. They can occur with sleep apnoea and shouldn’t simply be folded into a menopause explanation, though on their own they don’t establish a diagnosis.4 In women, tiredness, headaches and insomnia are more common signs of sleep apnoea than they are in men, and loud snoring is less common.5

Don’t drive if you feel sleepy.2

The aim isn’t to find one explanation at all costs. It’s to make sure the details that matter reach someone who can assess them. Your sleep is worth discussing even if you’re unsure of your stage, even without night sweats, and even if the answer isn’t clear straight away.
