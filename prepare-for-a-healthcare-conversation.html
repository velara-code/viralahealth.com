Getting the most from your appointment

A few notes in your own words can help you explain what matters. You don’t need a perfect medical history, or a diagnosis, to begin.

26 October 20265 min read5 references

In brief

  • One sentence about what you most need help with is a strong start.
  • Bring a short note, your medicines and two or three priority questions.
  • Before you leave, check the next step and who to contact.

There’s a lot to fit into an appointment: what you’ve noticed, the question you forgot last time, what you hope will happen next. Preparing can be simple. It’s there to make room for your concerns, not to set you another task to get right.

You might begin with one sentence: “The main thing I need help with today is…” Finish it however feels natural: “I’m waking in the night and struggling to function at work,” perhaps, or “My bleeding pattern has changed and I want it checked.”

Take this with you

Appointment guidance from the NHS and the US National Institute on Aging suggests bringing a few things, so the time can focus on you:1–3

  • your main concerns, roughly when they began, and how they affect everyday life
  • any changes to your periods
  • an up-to-date list of medicines and supplements (packaging or photos are easier than remembering names)
  • any allergies and relevant medical history

Dates can be approximate. If a detail is unclear, say so rather than guess: “I think this started in spring” is perfectly useful.

Choose your priorities

Write down the two or three questions that matter most. One might be about what could explain the changes; another about the assessment or support available. You can mention what you’ve already tried and why it didn’t work for you, though you don’t need to have tried anything first.1,3

Your priorities belong in the conversation. Perhaps you most want to understand a new symptom, manage something that’s interrupting everyday life, or know which changes should bring you back. You can name the question without proposing its answer.

Make the appointment work for you

If you’d like someone with you, ask about bringing a trusted person for support or to take notes. Ask in advance about interpreting or any other communication support you need. For a phone or video appointment, check the instructions and your equipment, and keep your notes within reach. Arrangements differ between services and countries, so check with the team you’re seeing.1–4

Leave with a next step you understand

If something is unclear, ask for it to be explained again. Before the appointment ends, check what has been agreed, whether any tests or follow-up are planned, how results will reach you, and who to contact if they don’t arrive or your symptoms change. You can ask for written information, or jot down a few words of your own.1,2,5

Preparing helps communication; it can’t guarantee an answer in one visit. You’re allowed to bring uncertainty with you. A good outcome is a next step you understand, including what remains unclear and how it will be followed up.
